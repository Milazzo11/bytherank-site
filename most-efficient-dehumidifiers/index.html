How this ranking is scored

We fitted two straight lines of certified IEF on certified capacity, one for portable spec sets up to 25 pints a day and one above (ENERGY STAR's efficiency classes up to 25 and from 25.01 to 50 pints a day, with IEF floors of 1.70 and 2.01 L/kWh), over every portable ENERGY STAR dehumidifier spec set certified at 12 pints a day or more (few certified sets and a unit-typo listing below that), then took the models furthest above their own class's line. The 25-pint step decides much of the order: from 25.01 pints the floor is 2.01, so a unit just above 25 pints that sits on that floor scores below its class line and can rank below a smaller unit with a lower IEF, even though it removes more water at a higher IEF; to compare across the two classes, use the cost per 100 pints column, which does not depend on the class. The score is that distance alone; raw IEF and capacity are shown, not scored. The five-year electricity cost is shown for each (yearly energy computed from certified capacity and IEF at DOE's 1,095 dehumidifying hours a year x current EIA U.S. average residential price x 5; we do not use the listed yearly figure because for some listings it does not match their own capacity and IEF).

  • IEF vs same-class models (100%): certified IEF against the IEF its own size class shows for that capacity (one least-squares line per ENERGY STAR class, up to 25 and above 25 pints a day): the classes have different efficiency floors, and bigger units tend to score higher IEF, so raw IEF favours size (higher is better) Computed: 100 * (Integrated Energy Factor / (1.8175 - 0.0006 * Certified capacity) - 1) if Certified capacity <= 25, else 100 * (Integrated Energy Factor / (2.0424 - 0.0001 * Certified capacity) - 1).
  • Integrated Energy Factor (0%): shown, not scored: liters of water removed per kWh including standby power (U.S. DOE test); scoring it again would favour the larger class, which the class lines exist to remove (higher is better)
  • Certified capacity (0%): water removed per day under the current DOE test (higher is better)
  • 5-year electricity cost (0%): shown, not scored (it follows from capacity and IEF, already scored): the yearly energy that certified capacity and IEF imply at DOE's 1,095 dehumidifying hours a year, priced at the current U.S. average residential rate (EIA) over five years; a total, so it grows with capacity (see the cost per 100 pints removed for a like-for-like figure) (lower is better) Computed: certified capacity (pints/day) x 0.473 L per pint / certified IEF (L/kWh) x 1,095 / 24 (DOE's annual dehumidification hours, 10 CFR 430 subpart B appendix X1) x EIA U.S. average residential price x 5.
  • Electricity cost per 100 pints removed (0%): shown, not scored (it follows from IEF): what the electricity costs to remove 100 pints of water at the current U.S. average residential rate (EIA), so units of different capacity compare like for like (lower is better) Computed: 100 pints x 0.473 L per pint / certified IEF (L/kWh) x EIA U.S. average residential price (no operating hours: it depends only on IEF and the rate).
  • A percentage against a fitted line is scored as 100 + that percentage, against the best on the page.

Size-neutral comparison:

  • IEF vs same-class models compares Integrated Energy Factor with one least-squares line per size class, split at 25 pints/day: Integrated Energy Factor = 1.8175 - 0.0006 × Certified capacity up to 25 pints/day (72 distinct certified spec sets) and Integrated Energy Factor = 2.0424 - 0.0001 × Certified capacity above it (79), fitted over all 151 distinct certified spec sets that qualify for this page (after the filters and merging), so each model is compared with what its size predicts within its class.

Each number is scored against the best on this page (best = 100%; e.g. 90% of the best capacity = 0.9) and multiplied by its weight; the total is out of 100. Full method.

FAQ

What is IEF?

Integrated Energy Factor: liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour, including standby power, under the U.S. DOE test procedure. Here it runs from 1.95 to 2.24 L/kWh.

Is a bigger dehumidifier less efficient?

Not across the board. ENERGY STAR's floor steps up from 1.7 L/kWh (up to 25 pints a day) to 2.01 from 25.01 to 50, so bigger units certify a higher IEF only across that step (the criteria set a third, higher floor above 50 pints a day; the largest portable certified in this pool is rated 50 pints a day); within each class IEF barely changes with capacity. That is why this page compares each model with its own class line; capacity here runs from 22 to 45 pints per day.

What does it cost to run one of these?

At the U.S. average residential electricity price of 0.1816 dollars per kWh (EIA, 2026-08), the yearly energy implied by certified capacity and IEF (1,095 dehumidifying hours a year, DOE) of the models on this page costs 219 to 399 US dollars over five years. Your state's rate and how many months it runs change this. The five-year figure is for the water each model removes at those hours, so a larger unit costs more in total; the cost per 100 pints removed compares them like for like, and a smaller unit may run longer in the same damp room.

Which one costs least to run per pint removed?

The Vellgoo TAURUS PRO-RCS50(Y)-N4-3-25: 3.84 US dollars of electricity per 100 pints removed at the U.S. average residential price (EIA, 2026-08); on this page the figure runs up to 4.41 for the ALORAIR Helios D35 and Nypeak D076A. It depends only on IEF, so it compares units across both size classes; the ranking above instead compares each unit with its own class line.

Where do these numbers come from?

Capacity and IEF come from the U.S. EPA ENERGY STAR certified Dehumidifiers dataset (retrieved 2026-11-01); we compute yearly energy use from them at DOE's 1,095 dehumidifying hours a year, not from the listed yearly figure, and take the electricity price from the U.S. EIA Electric Power Monthly (2026-08). We rank the certified results and compute the running cost; we did not run our own tests.
